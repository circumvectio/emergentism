# Four Statuses

There is a moment in most arguments about the infinite where everyone stops
meaning the same thing and nobody notices. It usually arrives within a minute.

The trouble is that we have one set of words — *endless*, *forever*, *infinite*,
*never gets there* — doing at least four separate jobs. The words are fine. The
jobs are not interchangeable. And because the words are the same, a claim about
one job can be answered with a fact about another, and both people can be
correct and still disagree.

So here are the four jobs, separated. You do not need any mathematics to hold
them apart. You need to notice that they answer different questions.

---

## The first question: is this even a legal move?

Before asking how big something is, or where it is heading, there is a prior
question that gets skipped constantly: **does the operation you are attempting
mean anything in the system you are in?**

Divide seven by zero. The answer is not a very large number. The answer is not
infinity. The answer is that ordinary arithmetic does not define this, and the
reason is not squeamishness. Division asks: *what number, multiplied by zero,
gives seven?* Nothing does — every number times zero is zero. So the question
has no answer, not because the answer is too big to write down, but because
there is nothing of the requested kind.

Call this status **undefined**. It is not a size. It is a report that the rules
have run out.

Everyday versions are easy to feel. *What is north of the North Pole?* is not a
hard geography question with a distant answer; "north" stops meaning anything
there. *What was the year before time started?* has the same shape. So does
*what colour is Thursday?* These are not mysteries. They are malformed requests,
and recognising a malformed request as malformed is an achievement, not a
failure.

This matters because **undefined is the status most often disguised as
infinity**, and the disguise is expensive. A system that reports "infinity"
where it should report "you asked me something I cannot answer" has converted a
clear signal into a confusing one.

There is a real subtlety here and it is worth having straight. Mathematicians do
sometimes extend a system so that previously undefined expressions get answers —
the projective line, for instance, adds a single point called infinity, and in
*that* system some divisions by zero become legal. That is not a discovery that
the old answer was wrong. It is a decision to work somewhere else, with
different rules and different costs. The move is legitimate. Making it silently
is not.

## The second question: can you list them all?

Suppose the operation is legal and you have a collection of things. Now ask:
**could you arrange them in a single queue — a first, a second, a third — such
that everything in the collection eventually appears?**

Not "could you finish the queue." You cannot finish an endless queue. The
question is only whether such a queue can be *set up* at all, with nothing left
out.

For the counting numbers, obviously yes: 1, 2, 3, and so on. For the even
numbers, also yes. For the prime numbers, yes — and there are endlessly many of
them, which Euclid proved around 300 BC by showing that any finite list of
primes can always be extended.

Here is the first genuinely surprising result. **The fractions can be queued
too.** Every fraction — a half, seventeen thirds, ninety-nine over a hundred —
can be put in a single list with nothing omitted. This feels wrong, because
between any two fractions there are endlessly many more, and it seems as though
that should defeat any queue. It does not. Georg Cantor showed how to build the
list in 1874. The trick is to stop trying to queue them in order of size and
queue them by something else entirely.

Call this status **endlessly listable**. There is always another one. There is
no last one. And yet everything has a place in line.

Now the harder case. Take all the numbers between zero and one — not just the
fractions, but every decimal expansion, including the ones that never repeat and
follow no pattern. Cantor proved in 1891 that **no queue can hold them all.**
Give him any list, however ingenious, and he will construct a number your list
missed: go down the list, and build a new decimal that differs from the first
number in the first place, the second in the second place, and so on forever.
The result differs from everything on the list, by construction. So the list was
incomplete. And this works against *every* list.

Call this status **beyond listing**. Not "a lot." Not "more than we can count in
practice." No possible queue, however clever, catches all of them.

The distinction is not about effort or lifespan. Both collections are endless.
One can be put in a line and one cannot, and that difference is exact, provable,
and has nothing to do with how much time anyone has.

One honest note, so that this does not sound tidier than it is: *beyond listing*
is not the top of anything. Cantor also proved that for any collection whatever,
there is a strictly larger one. There is no biggest infinity. The four statuses
here are a working distinction for ordinary confusions, not a complete census of
sizes.

## The third question: is it going anywhere?

The first two questions were about collections. This one is about **processes** —
a sequence of steps, one after another, with no last step.

Ask: **as the steps go on, do the results settle toward one definite value —
close enough that you could name any tolerance you like, however small, and the
process would eventually get inside it and stay there?**

If yes, the process is **headed somewhere definite**, and the place it is headed
is an ordinary, finite value.

Take one half, then three quarters, then seven eighths, then fifteen sixteenths.
Endless steps. Never a last one. And yet the results are closing on 1, and they
close on it as tightly as anyone could demand — name a margin, and past some
point every step is inside it. The process is endless; the destination is one
number, and a perfectly ordinary one.

Zeno of Elea built a famous puzzle on this around the fifth century BC: to cross
a room you must first cross half of it, then half of what remains, and so on
without end, so how can you ever arrive? The steps really are endless. But their
lengths shrink fast enough that they are headed somewhere definite — the far
wall — and the total distance is finite. Endlessly many steps do not require
endless distance, and they do not require endless time.

This is the point on which the argument in Chapter 1 turns, and it is worth
saying flatly: **an endless process and an endless result are different things.**
The digits of 0.999… are endless. The number those digits name is 1, exactly and
without shortfall. The endlessness is a property of the writing, not of the
value.

If the answer is no — the results do not settle — then the process is simply
**endless without settling**, and that comes in at least two flavours worth
telling apart. Some processes run away: 1, 2, 3, 4, and off it goes, never
approaching any value because it leaves every value behind. Others wobble
forever without running away: 1, then −1, then 1, then −1, staying in a small
region but never homing in on anything. Both fail to arrive. They fail
differently, and confusing "runs away" with "wobbles" is its own small error.

## The four, together

| Status | The question it answers | Short form |
|---|---|---|
| **Undefined** | Is this operation legal in the system I am in? | The rules ran out |
| **Beyond listing** | Could a single queue hold all of them? | No queue catches them all |
| **Endlessly listable** | Could a single queue hold all of them? | Always another; no last; all in line |
| **Headed somewhere definite** | As the steps go on, do the results settle? | Endless steps, one finite destination |

The reason these get confused is that ordinary English uses *infinite* for the
middle two and *never gets there* for the last one and *infinity* for the first
one, and so a single sentence can slide between all four without any visible
seam.

---

## How to sort one

A short procedure, in order. The order matters — later questions presuppose
earlier ones.

**1. Is the operation legal here?**
If the rules of the system do not define it, stop. The answer is *undefined*.
Do not upgrade it to a size. If you want an answer, you must move to a different
system, on purpose, and say so.

**2. Am I asking about a collection, or about a process?**
A collection is a set of things. A process is a sequence of steps. Many arguments
are one person holding a collection and the other holding a process.

**3. If it is a collection: could a single queue hold all of them?**
Yes — *endlessly listable*. No — *beyond listing*. Notice that both answers are
compatible with "endless." That compatibility is where most of the trouble
lives.

**4. If it is a process: do the results settle toward one value, to any tolerance
I care to name?**
Yes — *headed somewhere definite*, and the destination is finite and ordinary.
No — *endless without settling*, and it is worth saying whether it runs away or
wobbles.

**5. Say which one you meant.**
Most of the value is here. The four statuses are not hard to understand. They
are hard to keep separate under the pressure of a live argument, because the
same words serve all four, and the person you are talking to may be holding a
different one.

## Where this does not help

A tool that never fails is not a tool; it is a slogan. So, plainly:

This does not resolve every paradox. Some famous ones are not status confusions
at all. Russell's paradox — the trouble with the collection of all collections
that do not contain themselves — is not fixed by sorting endlessness properly.
It is a problem about self-reference and about which collections are allowed to
exist, and the repair came from restricting how collections may be formed, not
from distinguishing four kinds of endless. Anyone who tells you otherwise is
selling something.

It does not settle questions about the physical world. Whether space is
endlessly divisible in fact, as opposed to in our arithmetic, is a question for
physics, and the four statuses have no vote in it.

It does not tell you which status is *true* of a thing you have not examined. It
tells you which question you are asking. That is less than a theory and more than
a vocabulary, and it is the honest size of the claim.

And none of this is new. Every mathematical statement in this chapter was
established by someone else, most of it more than a century ago, and Chapter 8
is devoted to saying exactly who. The distinction is not a discovery. It is a
piece of ordinary equipment that, for reasons worth examining, mostly stayed
inside mathematics instead of coming out.

What follows is what happens when it does not come out — when systems large
enough to matter run endlessly without anyone asking where they are headed.
