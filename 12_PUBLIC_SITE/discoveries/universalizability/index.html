Run any maxim through a demanding inherited question: what if everyone did this? The test is associated with Kant, credited here. Emergentism adds a conditional model: repeated extraction among agents coupled to one degradable substrate undermines the conditions of its own continuation.

The discovery

Symbiosis can scale. Pure extraction consumes its conditions.

Take the extractor's strategy seriously—call it demon-polar: grow yourself by imposing loss on coupled others. It can reward an individual, sometimes for a long time. But where everyone depends on one finite, degradable substrate, repeated extraction erodes the hosts, trust, or ecology that later extraction requires. The result depends on those coupling and renewal conditions.

Now test a mutual-potential strategy: accept only moves that preserve or raise both sides under the Justice envelope. It does not guarantee utopia; coordination can still fail and costs remain real. Its narrower advantage is that success need not delete a victim or substrate as a precondition. That conditional asymmetry is the framework's “one bit.”

Credit where it is due: the universal-law test is associated with Immanuel Kant's categorical imperative, not invented here. We derived no new moral law. Emergentism translates one version of the test into a player–whole audit and adds explicit dynamical assumptions. The resemblance is a crosswalk to examine, not independent proof that the framework is true.

And one more honesty, the most important on this page. The test probes whether a maxim can be willed as universal; our dynamical model separately asks what persists under stated conditions. Neither forces you to care. Hume's gap stays open. The framework then takes a vow: refuse the move that destroys another impact-bearer's durable potential for your gain. A vow is not a theorem.

The is–ought gap stays open. One bit names the chosen direction; the Justice envelope carries the verdict.

Why this matters to you

It does not replace ethics. It gives you a ten-second first screen for a habit, policy, price, or prompt: ask “what if everyone did this?” Then do the slower work the bit cannot do—identify every impact-bearer, payer and beneficiary; check consent, custody, reversibility, contest, and exit. The first question is associated with Kant. The envelope and explicit shared-game assumptions are this framework's additions.
