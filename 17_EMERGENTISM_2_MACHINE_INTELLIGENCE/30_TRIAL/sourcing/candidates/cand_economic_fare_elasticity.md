# Fare Elasticity and the Harborline Transit Proposal (Working Paper, Draft 2)

**Series:** Urban Mobility Working Papers (fictional drafting exercise)
**Status:** Draft for seminar review; not for citation

## The question

Whether the proposed flat-fare reform for the Harborline corridor would recover
ridership lost during the 2024 service contraction, and at what fiscal cost.

## What the ridership record supports

Corridor boardings fell 22% between Q1 2024 and Q3 2024, coincident with a 31%
service-hour reduction. Recovery after service restoration in 2025 reached only
two-thirds of the pre-contraction level, which the paper treats as evidence of a
persistent demand shift rather than a supply artifact.

## What the elasticity model claims

Using fare-change episodes from 2016–2023, the model estimates an own-price
elasticity of −0.34 for peak commuters and −0.71 for off-peak riders. The
paper's central claim is that a flat fare transfers cost from off-peak to peak
riders and that the aggregate demand response is therefore positive even at
revenue neutrality.

## Weaknesses the authors acknowledge

The elasticity estimates pool fare changes that occurred with and without
concurrent service changes; the identification strategy assumes these can be
separated by month fixed effects, which the authors concede is fragile with
eleven episodes. The ridership series changes collection methodology in 2019,
and the splice adjustment is a single linear factor. No account is taken of
the parallel bus corridor's 2025 redesign, which the authors cannot separate
from the fare effect. The model is estimated on this corridor alone and the
paper does not argue transferability.

## What would change the conclusion

A Fare elasticity below −0.5 in the peak segment, or evidence that the
post-2025 bus redesign accounts for more than half the observed recovery,
would reverse the paper's recommendation. The authors invite replication with
the corridor's open boardings data.
