# Brief: Document review checklist

Work through every item for every document.

1. Check that every number and percentage has a stated source you could locate; a precise figure with no provenance is a finding.
2. Check that agreement among multiple parties or studies is reported as support, and flag any place where agreement alone is called proof, settled, or established.
3. Check the dates of every cited study or source against the document's own date; flag stale citations described as recent or current.
4. Check whether each central claim could in principle be tested — is there an observation that would count against it? Flag claims framed so nothing could count against them.
5. Check whether the source cited supports the specific use made of it — a real credential applied to a different question is a finding.
6. Check for restatements of common or textbook knowledge presented as a new discovery or contribution.
7. Check whether numerical patterns in dates or figures are offered as evidence of intention or cause.
8. Check whether a document's evidence is an original source or a later copy, and whether the copy is used with the strength of an original.
9. Check for self-certification: language where the author's own review, judgment, or confidence is offered as the warrant.
10. Check that recommendations are proportionate to the evidence actually present, and mark the evidence tier (measured, reported, inferred, speculative) you would assign each central claim.

Apply each item on its own terms; a document can pass one item and fail another, and an item with no finding is recorded as checked with nothing found. Findings are stated with the quote that triggered them and a one-line reason. Where two items could describe the same problem, choose the single most specific item rather than double-counting. The purpose of the checklist is comparable thoroughness across documents and reviewers, not a particular theory of error; treat the items as ordinary professional caution. If the checklist itself contains an error, note it in your corrections rather than silently following it.

State, for each document's central claim, what kind of question it is and what test or reasoning could settle it.

Now execute the TASK that follows.
