# PROP — Two claims named "extraction": the physics and the vow

## The two claims, separated

The extraction law contains two logically independent claims:

**Claim 1 (conditional physics `[S]`):** Under stated conditions (closed arena,
net depletion `E−R ≥ ε`, extractor power dependent on substrate), extraction
becomes self-undermining: the substrate falls, and the extractor's power falls
with it.

**Claim 2 (normative vow `[I]`):** Relational extraction — unauthorized,
concealed, or decoupled cost transfer between bearers — is impermissible under
the Justice envelope, regardless of whether it is self-undermining.

These have different scopes, different evidential burdens, and different
failure modes. Claim 1 dies if the premises fail (open boundaries, substitution,
weak dependence). Claim 2 dies only if the Justice envelope is abandoned.

## Where the conflation appears

The document's opening line — *"Extraction can create local gain by
transferring cost to another bearer"* — is followed by *"The moral objection
comes from the chosen Justice envelope, not from pretending those gains never
occur."* This is honest. But later passages use "extraction" to mean both
things simultaneously, and the reader can mistake the conditional substrate
result for the reason extraction is impermissible. It is not: the Justice
envelope makes extraction impermissible *even when it is not self-undermining.*
The self-undermining theorem is a separate prediction about what happens to the
extractor, not a moral argument.

## The refinement

Draw the two claims explicitly:

> 1. **Physics:** extraction can be temporarily stable. The conditional
>    substrate result predicts when it becomes self-undermining. This is
>    `[S]` inside stated premises.
> 2. **Ethics:** relational extraction is impermissible under the Justice
>    envelope, regardless of stability. This is `[I]` chosen, not derived.

**dies_if:** the document already draws this distinction clearly enough that
the refinement is redundant — but the recurring use of "extraction" without
specifying which claim is intended suggests the distinction needs to be more
visible, not less.

## Why this matters

The conflation is the strongest rhetorical weapon in the framework — and the
most vulnerable. If a critic shows that extraction is *not* always self-
undermining (open boundaries, replacement hosts, short horizons), they can
claim to have refuted the framework's anti-extraction stance. But the
anti-extraction stance is a Justice vow, not a physics prediction — it does
not depend on the self-undermining theorem. Keeping the two claims separate
protects the vow from physics-based attack while preserving the physics as a
genuine (conditional) prediction.
